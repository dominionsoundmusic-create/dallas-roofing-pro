Flat roofs are more common on DFW houses than people think

Ask around a Dallas neighborhood and plenty of homeowners own a flat roof without calling it that: the covered patio built off the back of a brick ranch, the den or garage apartment someone added in the 1980s, the carport, the porch roof under a second-floor window, or the whole roof of a mid-century modern house. Flat roof repair in Dallas is mostly about those low-slope sections, plus the duplexes, small shops and offices with the same kind of roof.

"Flat" is really "low slope." The 2021 International Residential Code sets a minimum design slope of one-quarter inch per foot (2 percent) for modified bitumen and thermoplastic single-ply roofs, just enough to move water toward a drain, gutter or scupper. When that small pitch is lost to a sagging joist or a bad tie-in, water stays put.

A drainage checklist you can run from the ground

Most flat roof trouble starts with water that has nowhere to go. You do not need to go up to spot the warning signs. Look from the yard, a second-story window or inside the house the day after a good rain, then again two days later.

  • The two-day test. NRCA calls it ponding when a puddle is still there two full days after the rain stops, in weather that should have dried it. If you can see a puddle from an upstairs window on day three, note where it is and photograph it.
  • Downspouts and scuppers. During a rain, is water actually coming out of the downspout or through the opening in the parapet? A dry downspout in a downpour points to a clog above.
  • Overflow stains. Dark streaks running down brick or siding below the roof edge mean water is spilling over instead of draining.
  • The tie-in line. Inside, check the ceiling where the flat section meets the main house. Stains along that line usually mean the flashing between the two roofs has failed.
  • Sagging ceilings or doors that stick. A deck that has deflected can trap water and in turn add more weight. Keep people out from under a bulging ceiling.
  • Debris after wind. Leaves, pecan hulls and shingle grit wash onto low sections from the roof above and pack into drains. Ask the roofer to clear them on each visit.

The International Building Code describes positive roof drainage the same way: the roof must be built with enough slope, allowing for how the deck bends under load, to drain within 48 hours of precipitation. For an existing roof, the residential code lets a re-roof skip the quarter-inch minimum slope only when the roof already drains that way.

Where flat roofs fail on North Texas homes

Seams and laps

Every membrane has joints. Welded TPO seams can be missed in spots, adhesive seams on EPDM age, and the laps on rolled asphalt roofing open up after many summers of expanding and contracting.

Blisters and cracks

Trapped moisture or air under asphalt-based roofs swells into blisters in summer heat. A blister is not always a leak, but one that splits lets water into the layers below.

Drains and scuppers

Low roofs collect what falls from higher ones. A clogged drain or a scupper set too high leaves a pond the size of the clog's reach.

The wall and roof joint

Where a patio roof or addition meets the brick or the main roof, the flashing has to turn up the wall and tuck under the siding or shingles. Poor detailing here is a classic source of leaks in additions.

Penetrations

Vent pipes, skylights, satellite mounts and the occasional window unit stand or AC line set punched through a flat roof all need their own flashing.

Hail and foot traffic

Low roofs take hail straight on, and every service visit to a rooftop unit adds wear. Look for punctures and torn spots near equipment after storms.

Flat roof repair, do it yourself? Here is why not

A flat roof looks easy to walk on, and hardware stores sell cans of patch. That combination sends a lot of people up a ladder. The risk is real: OSHA's residential construction guidance says falls are the leading cause of work-related deaths among residential construction workers, and its fall prevention campaign reports that falls to a lower level caused 389 of the 1,034 construction deaths recorded in 2024. Those are trained workers with equipment. A wet membrane, a soft deck around an old leak or a step back off an unguarded edge does not care how flat the roof looks.

The repair itself is also harder than it seems. A patch has to match and bond to the existing material, which is often unknown to the homeowner. Some low-slope systems go down with hot asphalt or an open torch. A sealant smeared over a seam can hide where the water actually enters and make the real repair larger later. And finding the leak is half the job: water on a flat roof can travel a long way under the membrane before it shows on the ceiling.

A local problem on a roof that is otherwise dry and sound is usually a repair: re-welding or re-taping a seam, rebuilding flashing at a wall, clearing and resetting a drain, or patching a blister. When water has soaked the insulation or decking across a wide area, or the surface is brittle and cracked everywhere, replacement starts to make more sense.

Low spots that cause ponding often need more than a patch. A roofer may suggest tapered insulation or a cricket to steer water toward the drain, or fixing a sagging joist before new roofing goes on. Ask for the drainage fix to be written as its own line on the estimate.

What to ask before a flat roof repair

  1. What is the roof made of now? TPO, EPDM, modified bitumen, rolled roofing or a coating. The repair material should be compatible with it.
  2. Where is the water getting in? Have the roofer photograph where water enters the roof itself, not only the ceiling stain.
  3. Is the insulation or deck wet? A few core cuts or a moisture reading answers whether a repair will last.
  4. What will fix the ponding? Drain work, tapered insulation, a cricket or structural repair, each priced separately.
  5. How is the flashing at the wall handled? It should run up the wall and tie in under the siding, brick flashing or shingles, not stop at a bead of caulk.
  6. Is a permit needed? Many DFW cities require one for re-roofing; small repairs often do not need one. Ask the roofer to check with your city.
