The short answer, then the North Texas fine print

A roof in Texas lasts roughly 20 to 30 years if it is asphalt shingle, 40 to 80 years if it is metal, and a century or more for clay or concrete tile, going by the estimates InterNACHI publishes for normal wear. Those are averages for a roof that is installed well, ventilated well and spared a major storm.

That last condition is the hard one in Dallas-Fort Worth. The region has a long record of damaging hailstorms, and a summer stretch of 100 degree afternoons bakes a roof from above while a poorly vented attic cooks it from below. A roof here can need replacing long before its chart age, not because the shingle was bad but because a storm or the heat got there first.

So the better question is not "how old is my roof?" but "what has my roof been through, and what does it look like now?" This guide gives you the published numbers, what local weather does to them, the signs you can check without setting foot on a ladder, and how roof age plays into homeowners insurance.

Published life spans by roofing material

The life column comes from InterNACHI's standard estimated life expectancy chart and its roof inspection course material. InterNACHI says the figures assume recommended maintenance and normal wear and are guidelines, not promises. The other columns describe how each material fares under DFW weather.

Flat and low-slope roofs on porches, additions and businesses use membranes with their own ratings, so ask the roofer for the membrane type, its thickness and the maker's expected service life.

The National Association of Home Builders reached the same caution in its study of how long home components last: life depends on how well a part was installed, how it was maintained, the weather it faced and how hard it was used. A number in a chart is where the conversation starts, not where it ends.

A warranty term is not a life expectancy

Shingle packaging talks in decades, and "lifetime" is printed on most architectural lines sold today. NRCA's Professional Roofing magazine has pointed out that a warranty's headline length adds together a non-prorated period and a prorated one, and that a lifetime label does not promise the roof will perform for a lifetime. The details live in the warranty document, not on the bundle wrapper.

Take GAF's standard lifetime shingle warranty as an example of how one major maker structures it (this is product information, not an endorsement). Its full-coverage period is 10 years, during which the maker supplies replacement materials, with labor, for a manufacturing defect. After year 10, labor drops off and material coverage is prorated. "Lifetime" means as long as the original owner owns the single-family home. Higher tiers stretch the full-coverage window, usually with conditions about which accessories are used and who installs them.

  • Manufacturing defect, not weather. Hail damage and wind stronger than the shingle's rating are generally matters for your homeowners policy, not the shingle warranty.
  • Registration matters. Some enhanced warranties exist only if the roofer registers the job. Ask for proof at closeout.
  • Transfer rules. If you sell, the next owner may get reduced coverage or none. Read the transfer section before you count the warranty as a selling point.
  • Workmanship is separate. Installation errors are covered, if at all, by the roofer's own written workmanship warranty. Get its length and terms on paper.

What DFW weather does to those numbers

Hail, the biggest variable

The National Weather Service counts hail of one inch or larger as severe. In North Texas, storms well past that size are part of the record: the April 11, 2016 supercell that hit Wylie damaged roughly 80 percent of the city's homes, by the Fort Worth NWS office's account, and the 1995 Mayfest storm dropped softball-size hail on Fort Worth. A roof does not have to look shredded to be shortened. Hits that crack the mat or knock off granules can leave a roof that keeps out water today and fails years early.

Heat and sun

The Fort Worth NWS office puts the DFW average at about 20 days a year at 100 degrees or higher, based on 1991 to 2020 normals, almost all of them from June through September, and 2011 logged 71. Asphalt shingles age through heat and sunlight; granules shield the asphalt, and as they wear away the shingle dries, curls and turns brittle faster.

Wind after the hail

Wind research from IBHS (Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety) points to the strength of a shingle's sealant bond as critical to resisting wind, especially after hail. A roof that rode out a hailstorm may lose tabs in a later straight-line wind event because the seal was already weakened. That is one reason a roofer looks at the whole roof after a storm, not just the spot that leaked.

Rain in spring and fall

DFW averages about 37 inches of rain a year, with May the wettest month at close to five inches, according to NWS normals. Heavy spring rain follows the hail season, so a bruise or lifted shingle from April often turns into a ceiling stain by June. Water that sits behind clogged gutters also soaks the roof edge and the decking beneath it.

The attic sets the pace from underneath

{{ img('attic-roof-decking-flashlight-inspection.jpg', 'Person in an attic shining a flashlight along the roof rafters and decking', 1200, 800) }}

A shingle roof sits on top of the attic, and the attic's temperature affects it. The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association says ventilation helps prevent premature shingle deterioration by keeping the attic closer to the outside temperature, and that it also carries off moisture that drifts up from the living space. Its guidance calls for balance: intake vents low at the eaves and exhaust vents high near the ridge, so air keeps moving through.

The residential building code sets a minimum amount of net free vent area for a vented attic, generally 1 square foot for every 150 square feet of attic floor, with a reduced ratio allowed only in listed cases. ARMA treats code as a floor, not a target. Common trouble in DFW houses includes soffit vents painted shut or buried under insulation, a mix of exhaust types fighting each other, and bath exhaust ducts that end in the attic rather than at an outside vent, which loads the space with moisture.

Check whether your shingle warranty sets ventilation requirements, because a hot, stuffy attic can cost you twice: a shorter roof life and a weaker warranty position. When a roofer prices a replacement, ask how it calculated the intake and exhaust and whether the bid brings the attic up to that number.

Signs a roof is near the end, checked from safe ground

You can learn a lot without climbing anything. Never go up on the roof to look; a roofer has the footing, the gear and the training. Here is what consumer guides from the insurer Amica and from Angi list as signs worth a professional look:

From the yard and driveway

  • Shingle edges curling up or corners cupping, easiest to see in low morning or evening light.
  • Bald, dark patches where the colored granules have worn away.
  • Cracked, missing or mismatched shingles, especially near ridges and edges.
  • A sagging line along the ridge or a dip between rafters.
  • Granules piling up at downspout outlets. Some shedding is normal on a brand-new roof; heavy loss on an older one is not.

Inside the house

  • Water stains on ceilings, especially near outside walls or below chimneys and vents.
  • Peeling paint or a musty smell in a top-floor room or closet.
  • From the attic hatch, with a flashlight and without crawling across joists: daylight through the decking, dark stains or damp insulation.
  • Higher cooling bills along with a very hot upstairs, which can point to ventilation trouble.

One sign on its own often means a repair. Several together, on a roof near the end of its published range, usually mean it is time to plan a replacement on your schedule rather than wait for a leak to set it. Write down what you see, take photos from the ground, and note the year the roof went on if you know it; the roofing company you talk to will want all three.

How insurers treat an older roof

Roof age matters to your homeowners policy as much as to the roof itself. The Texas Department of Insurance cautions that renewal is when many changes happen: an insurer may shift an aging roof to actual cash value terms, and a roof already in bad shape can lose coverage. Some policies go further with a roof payment schedule, which pays a shrinking percentage of the roof's replacement cost as the roof gets older. Every policy is different, so none of this tells you what yours will do; it tells you what to look for.

TDI's own illustration shows why the coverage basis matters. It assumes a $10,000 roof claim on a $200,000 home with a 2 percent deductible of $4,000:

Roof age in TDI's exampleReplacement cost policy paysActual cash value policy pays
5 years$6,000$4,500
10 years$6,000$3,000
20 years$6,000$0

These are TDI's sample numbers, not a prediction for your claim. They show that on an actual cash value basis, depreciation plus the deductible can leave little or nothing for a roof near the end of its life.

What to ask your agent or insurer

  1. Which basis covers my roof? Replacement cost, actual cash value, or a roof payment schedule. Look at the declarations page and the endorsements, not only the policy summary.
  2. What roof age and material do you have on file? If the insurer thinks the roof is older than it is, or does not know it was replaced, send the paperwork.
  3. Did anything change at renewal? TDI suggests checking roof coverage each time the policy renews.
  4. What is my wind and hail deductible in dollars? A percentage deductible is figured on the dwelling limit, so do the arithmetic before a storm.
  5. Do you give a credit for impact-resistant roofing, and what do you need to apply it?

Class 4 shingles: longer life or fewer claims?

Impact-resistant shingles are tested under UL 2218. For the top rating, Class 4, a sample has to come through a two-inch steel ball dropped twice on the same spot. IBHS now runs its own tests with lab-made hailstones and rated 24 impact-resistant products in its 2025 round; it suggests choosing one that also scores Good or Excellent in its ratings, since a Class 4 label alone does not separate the strong performers from the weaker ones.

The honest way to think about them in DFW: they do not stop the sun and heat from aging asphalt, so the clock in the table above still runs. What they may change is how often hail ends that clock early. TDI says insurers credit roofing rated under UL 2218, with Class 4 earning the largest credit, though each company sets its own amount, and the roofer completes TDI form PC068 after installation as the record of what went on. Some policies that take the credit also carry an endorsement excluding cosmetic hail damage, so ask about that too.

Homeowners associations face a limit here: section 202.011 of the Texas Property Code restricts an HOA's power to bar shingles designed to resist wind and hail, provided they resemble the shingles allowed in the neighborhood and meet the quality and appearance tests in the statute. Ask the HOA for its written roofing standards before you sign.

Keeping a roof going after North Texas storms

The National Roofing Contractors Association recommends having a roof checked at least twice a year, in spring and fall, and after any severe storm, with owners looking only from the ground. In DFW that rhythm lines up well with the weather: a spring look catches hail and wind damage before the wettest weeks, and a fall look finds summer heat wear before winter cold snaps.

  1. After hail or high wind, walk the yard. Look for shingle pieces, granules in the downspout splash, dents in gutters, vents or patio furniture, and photograph everything with the date.
  2. Check ceilings within a day or two. Fresh stains, drips or a bubble in the paint mean water is getting in. Move belongings, set out a bucket and keep people out from under a sagging spot.
  3. Keep gutters and downspouts flowing. Have a gutter service or roofer clear them, especially under trees, so water does not sit at the roof edge.
  4. Trim limbs back from the roof. Branches that rub scrape off granules, and dead limbs come down in storms. Leave limbs near power lines to the utility or a qualified tree crew.
  5. Have small problems fixed while they are small. A lifted shingle, a cracked pipe boot or loose flashing is a modest repair; left alone through a wet spring, it can mean wet decking and insulation.
  6. Keep a roof file. The install date, invoice, permit, warranty registration and storm photos help with both an insurance claim and a home sale.

Mistakes that shorten a roof's life

  • Walking the roof to "take a look." Foot traffic cracks aging shingles and tile, and it is dangerous.
  • Letting a new layer go over an old one without asking. Code bars a re-cover over two existing layers or over wet or deteriorated roofing, and a tear-off lets the roofer see the decking.
  • Ignoring the attic. A new roof over the same hot, poorly vented attic starts aging at the same pace as the old one.
