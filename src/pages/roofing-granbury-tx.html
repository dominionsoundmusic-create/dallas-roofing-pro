Lake town, county seat, and a permit desk that checks names

Granbury is the seat of Hood County, a city of 10,958 people at the 2020 census that grew up around a courthouse square. Several buildings on that square, the Granbury Opera House among them, are registered historic landmarks. In 1969 a dam on the Brazos River created Lake Granbury, a long and narrow lake that winds right through town.

For roofing in Granbury, the fact that matters most is quieter than the lake or the square. The City of Granbury says only contractors and subcontractors registered with the city can obtain a building permit. That registration gives a homeowner something concrete to verify, which is rare in a state that does not license roofers.

Checking a roofer the Granbury way

These steps follow the City of Granbury's own permit page, its Building Inspections FAQ and its Residential Construction Guidelines.

  1. Ask if the company is registered with the City of Granbury. Registration comes before any permit. A firm that is not registered files an application with Building Inspections first.
  2. Confirm it with the city, not the salesperson. Building Inspections can tell you whether a contractor is registered and current.
  3. Ask whether your job needs a permit. The city's monthly reports show residential and commercial re-roof permits being issued. Find out who applies for yours.
  4. Check every name on the permit. Granbury's FAQ says the general contractor and all subcontractors must be registered, current and listed before the permit is issued.
  5. Keep the permit with your house papers. It records who did the work and when, which can help with a later sale or claim.

Homes outside the city limits, in other parts of Hood County, are not under the City of Granbury's permit desk. If you live outside town, ask the roofer which rules apply to your address and confirm with the county.

May 15, 2013: the night Granbury learned about roofs and wind

On the evening of May 15, 2013, a tornado struck the southeast part of Granbury. The National Weather Service office in Fort Worth rated it EF4, the strongest of that night's outbreak, with winds it estimated near 180 mph; early reports gave a range up to 200 mph. Six people died, all in the Rancho Brazos subdivision, where damage was put at about $143 million.

The same storm system dropped an EF3 tornado near Cleburne in Johnson County, and the NWS confirmed 16 tornadoes across North Texas within two days.

No roof is built to survive the core of an EF4. What that night left behind for Granbury owners is more practical: after a widespread storm, roofers, adjusters and door knockers arrive all at once. A homeowner who already knows to ask about city registration, insurance certificates and written scopes is in a better spot than one who signs with the first truck on the street.

Four reasons Granbury callers dial the line

Spring storms off the Brazos valley

Hood County sits in the path of the same spring supercells that cross the rest of North Texas. After wind or hail, owners want a roofer to look before the insurer's adjuster does.

Shoreline homes with nothing to slow the wind

Houses that face open water along Lake Granbury have fewer trees and roofs around them to break a gust, so loose ridge caps and lifted shingles show up there first.

Older homes near the square

Granbury's historic core holds homes far older than the newer subdivisions, and older roofs can hide tired flashing, multiple layers or damaged decking.
