A Dallas roof leak starts somewhere else

The stain on your ceiling is where the water finished, not where it started. Rain gets through at a gap on the roof, creeps beneath the wood sheathing or rides a rafter for a few feet, and falls onto the drywall wherever gravity lets go. That is why roof leak repair in Dallas begins with tracing the path, and why patching the shingles right above the stain so often fails.

The good news: most leaks come from a short list of weak spots, and most of them are small, local repairs on a roof that still has life in it. The longer a leak runs, though, the more wood, insulation and drywall get pulled into the bill.

The first hour: what to do while it drips

  1. Keep your feet on the ground. Wet shingles are slick, and the National Roofing Contractors Association tells owners to look at storm-hit roofs only from the ground.
  2. Protect what is under the drip. Slide furniture, rugs and electronics out of the way. Cover what cannot move with plastic sheeting.
  3. Catch the water. A bucket or pan with a towel in the bottom cuts the splash. Lay towels around it.
  4. Keep clear of a sagging ceiling. If drywall is bowing or a light fixture is dripping, keep everyone out of the room and switch nothing on there.
  5. Write down the details. Time, how heavy the rain was, wind direction, which room. A roofer uses those clues.
  6. Take photos. The ceiling, the drip, the room, and anything visible on the roof or lawn from the yard. Date them for any insurance question later.
  7. Call. A roofer for the source, and your insurer if a storm caused it.

Is it the roof, or something in the attic?

Many DFW homes have the air handler, ductwork and sometimes a water heater in the attic. Any of them can drip through a ceiling and look exactly like a roof leak. When the water shows up is the best clue.

Residential mechanical code calls for a backup to protect ceilings under cooling coils in attics: a secondary drain pan, a separate overflow line, or a float switch that shuts the system off when water rises. A secondary pan line is supposed to drain somewhere you will notice, often above a window. Water dripping from that spot on a dry day means the main line is clogged. Where water usually gets in on Dallas roofs

Around pipes and vents

The collar that seals a plumbing vent dries out and splits after years of sun. Exhaust vents and roof jacks loosen. Stains under these are often in a bathroom, laundry or hallway.

Where roof meets wall or chimney

Step flashing, counterflashing and chimney flashing turn water away from vertical surfaces. If a piece slips or was only caulked, water runs behind it. The flashing page shows where each piece belongs.

Valleys

The trough between two slopes carries the most water on the roof. Worn shingles, a badly cut valley or debris that dams the flow can send water sideways under the shingles.

Storm-damaged shingles

Hail fractures, wind creases and lifted tabs open a path that grows with each rain. If the leak appeared after a storm, read storm damage roof repair before you call the insurer.

How a roofer traces the leak

{{ img('attic-roof-decking-flashlight-inspection.jpg', 'Person in an attic shining a flashlight along the roof rafters and decking', 1200, 800) }}

A careful roofer works from both sides. Inside the attic, it follows water stains, dark wood and damp insulation uphill from the ceiling stain until the trail stops, and checks the air handler pan while it is there. On the roof, it looks at every opening, wall joint and valley above that point, not just the shingles directly overhead.

Request pictures of the entry point before and after the fix, and to note whether any decking was soft. A leak that ran for months may have rotted a section of deck, and that section should be cut out and replaced rather than shingled over.

Timing also matters here. National Weather Service normals for 1991 to 2020 put DFW at about 37 inches of rain a year, with May the wettest month and October next, so spring and fall are when a hidden gap gets tested hardest. A leak noticed in a dry August may be one that has dripped quietly since May.

Mistakes that turn a small leak into a big one

  • Waiting for the next storm to "see if it does it again." Each rain soaks the same insulation and decking a little more, and wet wood under shingles does not dry out on its own.
  • Accepting a patch right above the stain. If nobody traced the water uphill in the attic, the patch may sit nowhere near the real gap.
  • Tearing out wet drywall before taking photos. Pictures of the stain and the wet materials help both the roofer and any insurance question.
  • Covering a gap with roof cement and calling it done. Cement over a split boot or loose flashing is a stopgap. Make sure the invoice says whether the part was replaced or only sealed.
  • Paying in full before a good rain. Ask whether the roofer will come back if the same spot drips during the next storm, and get that promise in writing.

What shapes the price of a leak repair

The line never quotes prices; the roofer does, in writing, after finding the cause. These are the questions that decide how big the bill gets.

What failed

A new boot or a few shingles is small. Rebuilding flashing around a chimney or along a wall takes more time and metal.

How long it ran

Weeks of dripping can mean rotted decking and wet insulation. The EPA advises drying water-damaged materials within 24 to 48 hours to help prevent mold.

The roof itself

Steep, tall or multi-layer roofs take more labor. Discontinued shingles can make a matching patch harder.

Insurance is a separate question. Ask your insurer whether the cause, such as a hailstorm or wind, is covered under your policy and whether wear and tear is excluded, and how interior water damage is handled. The Texas roof claim guide lists what to ask.
