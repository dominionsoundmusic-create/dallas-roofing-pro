The short answer: wherever the roof is interrupted

Flashing goes anywhere water could slip past the shingles: where a roof meets a wall or chimney, where two slopes meet in a valley, along the edges, and around every pipe, vent and skylight that punches through. It is thin metal (sometimes rubber or a membrane) shaped to send water back onto the shingle surface.

  • Sidewalls: step flashing under each shingle course, with counterflashing or siding over it.
  • Chimneys: an apron at the front, steps along the sides, a back pan or cricket behind, and counterflashing set into the masonry.
  • Valleys: a metal or membrane lining in the trough.
  • Eaves and rakes: drip edge.
  • Roof eave against a wall: a kickout diverter.
  • Penetrations: pipe boots, vent flashing and skylight kits.

Where flashing is metal, the code calls for corrosion-resistant material no thinner than 0.019 inch, equal to 26-gauge galvanized steel (IRC R903.2.1). Your city enforces its own adopted edition, so section numbers can shift slightly.

How each kind of flashing is supposed to work

Step flashing and counterflashing

The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association describes step flashing as roughly 10-inch pieces, at least 2 inches wider than the shingle exposure, set one per course and nailed to the deck, never the wall. Siding should come down at least 2 inches over the vertical leg, acting as the counterflashing. On brick, a separate metal counterflashing is set into the mortar joint.

Chimney flashing and crickets

A chimney moves a little apart from the roof framing. ARMA's answer is two parts: base flashing fastened to the roof, counterflashing fastened to the masonry, so one slides over the other without opening a gap. It strongly recommends a cricket on any chimney 24 inches or wider; the IRC requires one above 30 inches.

Metal valleys

An open valley leaves a band of metal exposed down the center. ARMA lists 26-gauge G-90 galvanized steel, 16-ounce copper or 0.025-inch aluminum as minimums, and warns against shingle end joints in a valley. Closed-cut and woven valleys hide the trough under shingles instead. Each style works when installed to the shingle maker's instructions.

Drip edge

The metal lip along the eaves and the sloped rake edges. It keeps water from curling back onto the fascia and deck edge. The code wants it lapped at joints, fastened at close spacing, and set so underlayment runs over it at the eaves but under it at the rakes.

Kickout flashing

Where the lower edge of a roof dies into a wall (a garage roof against a two-story wall, for instance), the last step flashing must turn water out into the gutter. A missing kickout is one of the classic causes of rotted wall sheathing and stained interior walls below.

Pipe and vent flashing

Plumbing vents usually get a boot: a metal or plastic base with a rubber collar that hugs the pipe. Box vents and exhaust caps have their own flanges. The base slides under the shingles above it and laps over the shingles below, so water runs over it, never under it.

Why flashing wears out on North Texas roofs

{{ img('dented-gutter-hail-marks.jpg', 'Close view of a gutter and downspout corner on a brick home below a shingle roof edge', 1200, 800) }}

Heat cycling. The National Weather Service office in Fort Worth puts the DFW normal at about 20 days a year at 100 degrees or above (1991 to 2020), and 2011 logged 71. Metal on a roof heats and cools every day, and that movement works on nails, sealant and the joint between metal and brick. A flashing detail that relies on a bead of caulk instead of overlapping metal tends to open up first.

Rubber in the sun. Pipe boot collars and some sealants harden and crack with years of ultraviolet exposure. A split collar lets water run straight down the pipe.

Hail on soft metal. Aluminum and copper are softer than steel, and hail can dent valley metal, drip edge, vent caps and chimney counterflashing. A dent alone may be cosmetic; a crease that breaks a seam or lifts a lap is a leak path. After a storm, dented gutters, downspouts and vent caps seen from the yard are a sign to have the roof flashing checked. See hail damage roof repair.

Brick and siding work. New siding, stucco or a repointed chimney can bury or cut counterflashing. Ask whoever did that work whether the flashing was reset.

Signs of failed flashing, and what to ask the roofer

Clues you can spot safely

  • A ceiling or wall stain next to a chimney, dormer or second-story wall
  • Drips that appear only in wind-blown rain from one side
  • Rust streaks or gaps where metal meets brick, seen from the yard
  • Water staining on siding below a roof-to-wall corner
  • Dented vent caps or valley metal after hail

Questions for the roofer

  • Will you replace the flashing or just reseal it, and how long will each last?
  • Is the counterflashing cut into the mortar or only glued to the brick face?
  • Does this wall need a kickout, and is there one?
  • Will the shingles along the wall survive being lifted, or will some need replacing?
  • What metal and gauge are you using?

Many roofers check a leak at no charge, though not all do; ask during the call.
