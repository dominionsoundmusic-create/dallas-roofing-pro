The answer first, with names on every number

A full roof replacement in Dallas most often lands somewhere between about $6,000 and $14,000, according to Angi's 2026 cost data for the city, which puts the average near $10,054. A larger, fully specified re-roof costs much more: the Cost vs. Value report priced its model asphalt shingle job in Dallas at $25,274 in 2024.

Those two sources are not arguing with each other. Angi pulls from what homeowners report paying on all kinds of houses, from a small ranch in Pleasant Grove to a big two-story in Frisco. Cost vs. Value models a single large roof, skylights and all, and prices it as a full-service remodeler would. Your house could sit below, inside or above either one.

DFW Expert Roofers never sets, quotes or collects a price. The line answers your call and transfers it to an independent DFW roofing company, and that company climbs, measures and prices the roof itself. Real quotes vary, and two honest bids on one house can land thousands apart. The rest of this guide explains why, and what to check.

Why the Cost vs. Value number is so much higher

The Journal of Light Construction builds its model roof big on purpose: 30 squares of shingles (roofers count area in squares of 100 square feet each, so roughly 3,000 square feet), torn off down to bare sheathing, new underlayment, drip edge and flashing, a hip roof, a vented ridge and two skylights. Many DFW homes carry less roof than that, and skylights alone add a good deal of labor. The report is useful for one more reason: the resale column. In 2024 a Dallas re-roof was estimated to return under half its cost when the house sold, so a new roof is mostly a repair you need, not an investment that pays itself back.

Why the Angi ranges are so wide

Angi's ranges mix every size and shape of house and every grade of shingle. Its asphalt range alone stretches from under $8,000 to nearly $25,000. Use it to spot a bid that is far outside the usual band, then ask the roofer what makes your roof different. A good answer names something you can see: two layers, a steep pitch, lots of valleys, a chimney, rotten decking.

HomeAdvisor's Texas page also lists a Dallas average of about $9,500, but that page is several years old, so the newer Angi data is the better reference.

A bid that leaves several of these rows blank is not cheaper. It is incomplete, and the missing pieces tend to show up later as change orders.

Permits: a line item many DFW bids include

City rules shape both the price and the paperwork. The City of Dallas says replacing large areas of a roof, or the whole roof, requires a re-roof permit, while minor repairs to roofing or roof vents do not, and an emergency tarp needs no permit at all. If joists or rafters have to be replaced, Dallas requires a building permit instead. Permits are issued at the Oak Cliff Municipal Center, 320 E. Jefferson Blvd., Room 118, or online for registered contractors, and the city posts its fee schedule on the same Development Services site.

Neighboring cities draw the line in other places. Plano requires a permit for every re-roof and inspects the finished roof. Fort Worth says a shingle-only replacement on a one or two-family home does not need a permit, but replacing decking or structure does, and anyone pulling a Fort Worth permit must hold the city's contractor registration. Each DFW town page notes what that city's own site says.

Ask the roofer to name the permit in the contract, include its cost, and hand you the permit number. Skipping a required permit can come back to bite at closing or on a later claim.

Texas sales tax on a re-roof

Homeowners are often surprised to see tax on a roofing bill, or surprised not to. The Texas Comptroller's guidance on real property repair and remodeling explains both. Re-roofing a house counts as repair, not new construction, and labor to repair a home is not taxable. What happens with materials depends on the contract:

  • Lump-sum contract (one price for everything). The roofer pays sales tax on shingles and other materials when it buys them and does not add tax to your bill. That tax is part of the roofer's cost, so it is built into the single price.
  • Separated contract (materials and labor priced apart). The roofer buys materials tax-free with a resale certificate, then charges you state and local sales tax on the materials line, based on where your house is. The materials charge cannot be less than what the roofer paid.
  • Invoices do not change the contract. The Comptroller notes that separate invoices alone do not turn a lump-sum contract into a separated one unless the contract itself calls for them.

Neither type is better for you by default. What matters is that the contract says which one it is, so tax is not charged twice or added at the end as a surprise. Commercial roofs follow different rules; a building owner should ask an accountant.

Paying for it: deposits, final payment and financing

{{ img('roofing-contract-kitchen-table.jpg', 'Homeowner reading a printed roofing contract at a kitchen table beside a coffee mug', 1200, 800) }}

The payment schedule

The Federal Trade Commission's home improvement advice is blunt: hold back the last check until the job is complete and you are happy with it. A deposit toward materials is common, but paying most of the price before a shingle comes off leaves you with little leverage. Texas law also ties final payment on a home construction contract to a signed bills-paid affidavit from the contractor, stating that suppliers and workers were paid or listing who is still owed (Property Code 53.259). Ask for it with the final invoice.

Questions to ask about roof financing

  • Who is the lender, and is it the roofer or an outside company?
  • What are the APR and term, and what does the loan cost in total by the last payment?
  • Are there sign-up fees or a penalty for paying it off early?
  • Is the loan secured by your house or recorded as a lien?
  • How does it compare with a quote from your own bank or credit union?

The FTC warns about a contractor who arranges a loan, then has the homeowner sign papers mid-job that turn out to be a home equity loan. Never sign a document you have not read or one with blank spaces. If you signed a contract at home rather than at the seller's place of business, the FTC's Cooling-Off Rule generally gives you three business days to cancel.

When a storm claim is part of the cost

After hail or wind, the roof may be part of an insurance claim. How much of the bill a policy covers depends on whether it pays replacement cost or actual cash value, how large a wind and hail deductible you carry, and what the adjuster finds. None of that changes how the roof itself is priced: the roofer's estimate should still list every driver in the table above. Ask your insurer how it values the roof before you sign anything, and read the Texas roof insurance claim guide for the steps.

Five mistakes that cost DFW homeowners money

  1. Comparing totals instead of scopes. A lower bid that reuses flashing, skips the permit or leaves out decking is not the same job. Line the bids up against the driver table.
  2. Signing the night of a hailstorm. Crews from everywhere knock on doors after a big DFW storm. Take a day, get a second written bid, and check the company before you commit.
  3. Letting the decking price float. Without a per-sheet price in writing, the final bill can grow by an amount you never agreed to.
  4. Forgetting the warranty paperwork. At closeout, collect proof that the manufacturer warranty was registered plus the workmanship warranty on paper.
  5. Accepting a "we'll cover your deductible" offer. It is illegal in Texas, and an estimate padded to hide it puts you in the middle of insurance fraud.
