Two kinds of Waxahachie roofs

Waxahachie is the county seat of Ellis County and has carried the nickname "Gingerbread City" for the Victorian houses built during its boom years in the late 1800s and early 1900s. Its Richardsonian Romanesque courthouse still anchors downtown, and the West End Historic District, listed on the National Register in 1986, covers 77 acres of Queen Anne and Classical Revival buildings.

That is only half the city. The 2020 census counted 41,140 people, and the city's community profile estimated 53,000 as of June 1, 2026, which means thousands of newer homes on the edges of town. A roof on a 1990s ranch or a new subdivision house is a straightforward job. A roof on a turreted Victorian near the courthouse can bring historic review into the picture.

Roofing a house in a Waxahachie historic district

The city's historic preservation pages set out when the Heritage Preservation Commission gets involved. Here is how that plays out on a roof.

When a certificate is required

Under Section 24-56 of the city code, a Certificate of Appropriateness is required for alteration, restoration or new construction in a historic overlay district. The same review covers National Register properties and designated landmarks.

When a roof counts as maintenance

The city's historic tax incentive guidance lists roof replacement among routine maintenance that preserves a property without altering its historic appearance, and says routine residential maintenance does not need a certificate. Like for like is the safe reading.

When it becomes an alteration

Changing the roofing material, the color family, the roofline or adding features such as skylights changes how the house looks from the street. Ask the city before you sign a contract that includes any of those.

What skipping review can cost

For owners in the Historic Property Tax Incentive Program, the city says work completed without prior approval, however good the work, disqualifies the property from the program for that year.

Old houses also hide surprises a new subdivision home does not: multiple past layers, plank decking, chimneys with tired flashing and roof slopes that change across the house. Ask the roofer how it will inspect those before pricing the job.

Roofing work Waxahachie owners ask about

Requests range from a leak at an old chimney to a full replacement after hail, plus questions about metal roofs on rural property outside town.

Permits and paperwork at City Hall

  1. Call Building Inspection first. Waxahachie's building inspection staff answer at 469-309-4020. Ask whether your roof job needs a permit and, if the house is in a historic overlay district, whether planning staff need to see it.
  2. Check the roofer's registration. The city's permitting page includes a contractor registration form, which asks for the owner's driver license and proof of liability insurance of at least $300,000. Ask the roofer whether it has registered with Waxahachie.
  3. File through the CSS portal. The city takes applications, payments and inspection requests through its online Citizen Self Service portal.
  4. Expect the 2021 codes. Waxahachie adopted the 2021 International Codes, effective June 1, 2024. The city's own building guide repeats the code rule that asphalt shingles go only on slopes of 2:12 or steeper, with double underlayment on slopes from 2:12 to 4:12, which matters on porch roofs and additions with a low pitch.
  5. Keep the record. Hold on to the contract, any permit, the final invoice and the shingle warranty registration. On a historic home, keep any certificate as well.

The Saturday a tornado hit the new high school

Waxahachie High School had been open only since August when storms rolled through on Saturday, October 13, 2018. The National Weather Service confirmed a high-end EF-0 tornado that touched down just southwest of the school, with peak winds estimated near 85 mph in NOAA's storm record. Windows shattered, a door was busted, parking lot lights were bent and an old band bus ended up upside down in a nearby field. About 25 theater students were inside for a workshop, and no one was hurt.

An EF-0 is the weakest rating on the scale, yet it was enough to wreck a brand new building's doors and glass. On houses, winds in that range peel shingles and ridge caps and throw debris onto roofs. After any storm, look from the yard and from inside, and keep off the roof.
