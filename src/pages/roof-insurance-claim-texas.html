The short version

A roof insurance claim in Texas works like this: you report the damage to your insurer, an adjuster inspects the roof, the insurer accepts or rejects the claim within deadlines set by the Texas Insurance Code, and if it accepts, it pays according to your policy, after subtracting your deductible and, on many policies, depreciation.

Everything that matters to your wallet hides in the details of that sentence. Is your deductible a flat amount or a percentage of the home's insured value? Does the policy value the roof at replacement cost or actual cash value? Does it exclude cosmetic hail marks? Those answers are in your policy, and only your insurer can apply them to your claim.

This page explains the parts so you can ask the right questions. DFW Expert Roofers is not an insurance company, an adjuster or a roofer. You file and manage the claim yourself, or hire a licensed public adjuster.

The claim, start to finish

  1. Make the house safe and stop more water. Move belongings away from leaks and have a roofer cover any opening. Keep every receipt. According to the Texas Department of Insurance (TDI), finishing permanent repairs before the insurer views the damage can give it a reason to turn the claim down, so hold off on the full fix.
  2. Document from the ground and indoors. Date-stamped photos of dented gutters and downspouts, damaged screens, torn shingle pieces on the lawn, water spots on ceilings, and anything else visible from the yard or the attic hatch. Write down when the storm hit.
  3. Get an inspection. A roofer can look at the roof, photograph what it finds and write an estimate. That gives you something to compare with the adjuster's numbers later.
  4. Report the claim. Call your insurer or use its app, and get a claim number. Check your policy for a filing deadline; TDI notes that some policies allow one year.
  5. Meet the adjuster. The insurer sends someone to inspect. You can have your roofer present so the two look at the same damage. Ask the adjuster for a copy of the estimate.
  6. Get the decision. Texas sets deadlines for this part, covered in the next section. A rejection must explain why in writing.
  7. Compare and settle the scope. If the insurer's estimate misses items your roofer found, send the roofer's estimate and photos to the insurer and ask it to reconsider. Many policies also have an appraisal clause for disputes over the amount.
  8. Do the work, then close out. Pay your deductible, have the roof replaced or repaired, and forward the closing invoice to the insurance company when depreciation is being withheld. If you have a mortgage, expect the lender's name on the check.

Deadlines Texas puts on your insurer

Chapter 542 of the Texas Insurance Code, often called the prompt payment law, sets a clock for most first-party homeowners claims. TDI describes it this way.

StageWhat the insurer must doTime allowed
AcknowledgeConfirm it received your claim and start its investigationAbout 15 days after notice (TDI's consumer guidance counts business days)
DecideTell you in writing whether it accepts or rejects the claim, with reasons for a rejection15 business days after it receives the information it asked for
ExtendTake more time, if it tells you whyUp to 45 more days
PaySend payment after agreeing to pay all or part5 business days
Weather catastropheDeadlines may be stretched after a declared weather disaster15 more days

TDI says that if a company misses these deadlines, you may be able to collect the claim amount plus interest and attorney's fees. If a claim stalls or you think it was handled unfairly, the TDI Consumer Help Line is 800-252-3439. Keep a log of every call, email and letter with dates and names.

How the check is figured

Two policy terms do most of the work: how the roof is valued, and the deductible.

Replacement cost value (RCV)

The policy pays up to what it costs today to repair or replace the roof with similar materials, less your deductible. TDI explains that these claims usually come in two payments: the first is the estimate minus depreciation and the deductible, and the second releases the held-back depreciation once the insurer gets the bill for the finished work. That held-back amount is often called recoverable depreciation, and policies usually set a time limit for finishing repairs to collect it.

Actual cash value (ACV)

The policy pays the cost to replace minus depreciation for age and wear, less your deductible, and the depreciation is not paid back later. On an older roof the gap can be large. TDI notes that some insurers change an aging roof's coverage to ACV when the policy renews, and that a badly worn roof can fall outside coverage entirely, so check your renewal papers each year.

A worked example (made-up round numbers, not a price or a promise)

Say a home is insured for $300,000 with a 2% wind and hail deductible, which is $6,000. The adjuster approves an $18,000 roof estimate and figures $5,000 of depreciation for age.

Cosmetic damage, roof discounts and fine print

{{ img('phone-and-notes-on-desk-hero.jpg', 'Phone, notepad and coffee cup on a desk by a window looking out at a neighbor\'s house', 1920, 1080) }}

Hail can dent a metal roof or scuff a shingle without letting water in. Whether that is covered depends on your policy. TDI has long allowed an optional endorsement that excludes cosmetic hail damage when the policyholder gets a rate discount for an impact-rated roof, so a roof that earned you a discount may come with a narrower definition of damage. Find out from your agent if such an exclusion is attached to your policy, and what wording it uses for "cosmetic."

If you are thinking about impact-resistant shingles, TDI says each insurance company sets its own discount and paperwork. Ask what proof of the rating it needs, and keep the product labels and invoice.

Five questions for your insurer before you file

  • Is my wind and hail deductible a dollar amount or a percentage, and what is it in dollars?
  • Is the roof valued at replacement cost or actual cash value?
  • Is there a cosmetic damage exclusion or a separate roof endorsement?
  • What is the deadline to file, and to finish repairs and recover depreciation?
  • Will my mortgage company be named on the check, and how does it release the money?

Where Texas law draws the line for roofers on a claim

The state writes separate rules for contractors repairing damage that insurance money pays for. They exist to protect you, and a roofer who brushes them aside is showing you how it does business.

AllowedNot allowed
Look over the roof, take photos of damage and price the repairServe as your public adjuster, or advertise claim adjusting, for a home where it does or might do the roofing (Insurance Code 4102.163)
Share its estimate and discuss technical details with your insurer or adjusterNegotiate your settlement for you; only you or a public adjuster licensed by TDI may do that
Meet the adjuster at the house when you ask it toWaive, rebate or absorb your deductible, or pad the estimate to cover it (Business and Commerce Code 27.02)
Bill you for the deductible as part of the jobLeave the required deductible notice off an insurance-funded contract worth $1,000 or more
Ask for a reasonable payment schedule tied to the workAfter a declared disaster, if it has no local address of at least a year, require payment before work starts (Business and Commerce Code Chapter 58)

Two more points. Never sign your insurance check over to a contractor, and read anything a roofer asks you to sign after a storm; a document that hands the roofer control of your claim is a warning sign under the public adjusting rule. Report deductible offers to the Texas Attorney General's consumer line at 800-621-0508.

Claim mistakes that cost Texas homeowners

  • Waiting months to report. The longer between the storm and the claim, the harder it is to tie damage to one event, and your policy may have a hard deadline.
  • Letting the first estimate stand without reading it. Compare the adjuster's line items with the roofer's: squares, layers, drip edge, flashing, vents and permit.
  • Not knowing the deductible in dollars. A 2% deductible on a $400,000 home is $8,000. Check that before paying for anything.
  • Spending the depreciation check you have not received. On a replacement cost policy, the second check usually comes after the insurer sees the final bill.
  • Hiring the loudest door knocker. Check any roofer first. Texas has no state roofing license, so confirm the roofer's insurance certificate, permit and any city registration before you sign.
