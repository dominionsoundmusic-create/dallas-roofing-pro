Low rooflines and the sections nobody planned for

Picking a roofing company in Richardson often comes down to one question: can it handle the low parts? Much of the city was built out in the decades after World War II. D Magazine describes Canyon Creek as mostly ranch houses built from the late 1960s through the 1970s, and neighborhoods such as Greenwood Hills are known for their midcentury homes.

Those houses are handsome and long lived, but their roofs carry habits from another era. A main roof with a gentle pitch meets a patio cover that is nearly flat. A carport was enclosed into a den. A sunroom went on in the 1980s with a roof barely sloped enough to drain. Shingles were laid across all of it anyway.

Shingles shed water by gravity, and on a slope that is too shallow, wind-driven rain backs up under the laps. That is why so many Richardson leaks show up where a low section joins the main roof, not out in the open field of shingles.

Slope decides the material

The residential building code draws the lines. Ask each roofer to measure every section of your roof and write the material it plans for each one.

2 to 4 inchesShingles allowed with two layers of underlaymentLong, low midcentury main roofs and patio extensions

An estimate that lists "shingles, whole house" for a home with a nearly flat back porch deserves a second look. So does one that never mentions the joint where the two sections meet, since that seam needs proper flashing whatever goes on either side.

October 20, 2019: the tornado Richardson remembers

  • 8:58 p.m. The National Weather Service says the tornado touched down about 3 miles east-northeast of Irving, in northwest Dallas, and began tracking east-northeast.
  • Peak strength. Survey teams rated it EF3, with winds estimated at 140 mph and a path as wide as 1,300 yards.
  • 9:30 p.m. After about 15.8 miles it lifted roughly 2 miles southeast of Richardson. The weather service reported no deaths or injuries.
  • The count. Community Impact reported that Richardson assessed 3,117 properties. Of those, 671 had some damage and 12 were destroyed, with 54 homes and 196 apartment units left uninhabitable.
  • Recovery. The mayor signed a disaster declaration and the city waived building permit fees for tornado repairs. Crews hauled away more than 50,000 cubic yards of debris within weeks.

A year later, the Dallas Morning News found Richardson families still working through insurance and rebuilding. Roofs patched in that rush are now about seven years old. If yours was one of them, a careful look at the flashing, the decking and the repair seams is reasonable.

Checking a Richardson roofer with the city

Texas does not license roofing contractors. Richardson's own storm cleanup guidance points that out, noting that the state requires neither a license nor insurance for roofers. The city fills part of that gap with its own rules.

  1. Registration first. Every contractor must register with Richardson's Building Inspection Department before doing any work in the city. The fee is $100 and it runs one year.
  2. Look it up. The city keeps a Registered Contractors List and recommends checking it before you hire.
  3. Permit over 25%. Replacing, recovering or repairing more than a quarter of the roof needs a permit, approved and posted at the property before the first shingle comes off.
  4. Two inspections. One after the roof deck is installed, when decking is part of the job, and a final when the work is complete.
  5. Confirm the permit. The city's online permit search shows whether a permit was pulled for your address.

Building Inspection is at 2360 Campbell Creek Boulevard, Suite 550, and answers at 972-744-4180 on weekdays.
