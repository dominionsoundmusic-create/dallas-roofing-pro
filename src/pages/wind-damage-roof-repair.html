Wind is the storm damage people overlook

Wind damage roof repair in Dallas gets less attention than hail, yet North Texas thunderstorms regularly throw gusts strong enough to strip roofs. Straight-line winds and downbursts, the sudden rush of air that drops out of a strong storm and spreads across the ground, can flatten fences and trees over miles without a tornado anywhere nearby.

A shingle roof resists wind mostly through a strip of sealant that glues each course down to the shingles beneath. When gusts get under an edge and break that bond, the shingle flaps, creases near the nail line and may tear free. If it lies back down, the roof can look normal from the street while the next rain finds its way under the loose tab.

May 28, 2024: the windstorm Dallas remembers

The early morning storms of May 28, 2024 showed how much damage wind alone can do across the metroplex.

Hurricane-force gusts

Fox Weather reported a gust of 75 mph at Dallas Love Field and 83 mph near Denton. DFW Airport recorded 77 mph, according to CultureMap Dallas.

Damage across neighborhoods

Trees, fences and roofs were hit from downtown through North Dallas and into Irving. Dallas County Judge Clay Jenkins issued a disaster declaration as crews cleared the damage.

Record outages

PowerOutage.us counted close to 390,000 customers without power in Dallas County alone. Oncor later described it as the most impactful storm ever in its Dallas County service area, with 821,968 customers affected in all.

Many roofs damaged that morning were not obviously broken. Shingles that lifted and fell back, and ridge caps that loosened without blowing away, often showed up weeks later as leaks.

What wind does to a shingle roof at each speed

The National Weather Service uses 58 mph as its severe thunderstorm mark and, on many warnings, tags 70 mph winds as considerable and 80 mph winds as destructive. How a particular roof holds up depends on its age, its nailing and whether its shingles were still sealed, so read this as a rough guide.

Gust speedWeather service labelCommon roof damage
40 to 57 mphApproaching severeLoose or old shingles flap; tabs with failed sealant may crease; small limbs come down.
58 to 69 mphSevereRidge caps and edge shingles lift; scattered shingles go missing; larger limbs fall.
70 to 79 mphConsiderableWhole courses peel along rakes and eaves; flashing bends; trees fall on houses.
80 mph and upDestructiveLarge areas stripped to the felt or the deck; decking can lift on weak roofs.

Where wind gets a grip on a roof

Wind tunnel research by the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (IBHS) showed that the strength of the bond between shingles matters more than anything else in how a self-sealing shingle roof stands up to wind. In those tests, failures tended to start at shingles that were already partly unsealed and at the eaves and rakes, where the edge shingles are held mostly by their fasteners and starter strip.

Edges: eaves and rakes

Wind pressure is strongest along the edges. A drip edge that is too narrow or a starter strip that never sealed lets air under the first course, and the damage unzips from there. IBHS's FORTIFIED Roof standard calls for a wider drip edge and a fully adhered starter for this reason.

The ridge

Ridge caps sit at the highest, most exposed point and are bent over the peak, which stresses their seal. Missing caps leave the nail line and the ridge vent open to rain.

Creased tabs

A shingle that lifted and folded back carries a horizontal crease across its face. It may lie flat again, but the crease weakens it and the seal is broken, so it is likely to tear in the next gust.

Trees and debris

Many DFW streets are lined with tall pecans, oaks and elms. Falling limbs puncture decking, and branches dragged across the roof scrape away granules and break tabs.

A ground-level checklist after high wind

Everything below can be checked from the yard, the street or inside the house. Stay off the roof and keep the ladder in the garage.

  • Shingle pieces, ridge caps or strips of metal in the yard, the street or the neighbor's lawn.
  • Dark rectangles on a slope where shingles are missing and the felt or deck shows.
  • Shingle edges that look raised or wavy along the eaves and the sloped sides of the roof.
  • Gaps along the ridge line where caps used to be.
  • Gutters pulled away from the fascia, or flashing bent away from a wall or chimney.
  • Broken limbs resting on the roof or hanging over it.
  • New stains on upstairs ceilings, or light showing through the attic decking.

Photograph each item with the date, then have a roofer check the slopes in person.

Shingle wind ratings, decoded

Shingle wrappers carry wind ratings from two different tests, and they are easy to mix up. Neither one is a promise about any single storm, but both help when you compare products for a repair match or a new roof.

StandardHow it is testedClasses
ASTM D3161A sealed shingle panel is blown with a large fan for a set time.Class A (60 mph), Class D (90 mph), Class F (110 mph)
ASTM D7158Uplift is measured on the sealed shingle and wind resistance is calculated from the result.Class D, Class G and Class H, in rising order of resistance

Many architectural shingles carry both Class F and Class H. The rating only holds if the shingles are installed the way the maker specifies, with the right nails in the right place, so ask the roofer which nailing pattern it uses for that product.

How a roofer usually handles wind damage

  1. Find the full extent. The roofer checks every slope for creased and unsealed tabs, not only the bare spots, and looks in the attic for daylight or wet decking.
  2. Protect the opening. Where the felt or deck is exposed, a tarp or temporary cover keeps rain out until the repair.
  3. Match and replace. Damaged shingles come out, the surrounding tabs are lifted with care, and new shingles are nailed and sealed in. Ridge caps and edge metal are replaced where needed.
  4. Recheck the seal. Nearby shingles that lost their bond can be hand sealed. Ask whether that was done and where.
  5. Write it up. You should get photos of the damage and the finished work, plus an invoice that lists materials and areas repaired.

If the damage covers a large section, the City of Dallas and many other DFW cities require a permit. Ask the roofer whether yours does and who will get it.

Questions worth asking before the repair

  • Are there creased or unsealed shingles beyond the missing ones, and can you show me?
  • What wind rating does the replacement shingle carry, and does it match my existing roof?
  • How will the shingles around the repair be resealed?
  • Is my drip edge and starter strip in good shape, or part of the problem?
  • Will a permit be needed, and is it included in the price?
