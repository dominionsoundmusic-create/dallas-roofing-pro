What roof repair in Dallas usually means

Most roofs in Dallas that start leaking are not finished. They have one failure point: a rubber collar around a vent pipe that split after too many summers, a strip of metal at a wall that worked loose, or a handful of shingles that a spring gust peeled back. Fix that point properly and the roof can keep going for years.

This page is about those targeted fixes. It covers the problems roofers in Dallas find most often, how a careful roofer decides between patching and replacing, when the City of Dallas wants a permit, and what to ask before you agree to anything.

Six repairs Dallas roofers see again and again

North Texas weather works on a roof from several directions. The National Weather Service office in Fort Worth puts the normal at about 20 days a year of 100 degrees or more at DFW Airport, based on 1991 to 2020 records, and 2011 reached 71. Add spring hail and summer storm winds, and the same handful of weak spots keep showing up on service calls.

Split pipe boots

Plumbing vents pass through the roof inside a flexible collar. Sun and heat stiffen it until it cracks, and water runs down the pipe into a bathroom or hallway ceiling. On a sound roof this is one of the smallest repairs there is. The other boots are usually the same age, so ask about them too.

Loose wall and chimney flashing

Where a roof slope meets a brick wall, a chimney or a second story, thin metal pieces turn the water. When they slip, rust through or were only ever held with sealant, water gets behind them. Expect these drips to appear when rain blows sideways against that wall, then vanish in a calm shower. See roof flashing repair.

Lifted or missing shingles

Each shingle is nailed and also stuck to the course below by a sealant strip. A strong gust can break that bond. Some shingles blow off; others fall back flat and look fine while rain runs under them. A roofer checks the neighbors of every missing piece for broken seals. More on wind damage.

Hail bruises and cracks

Hail can knock granules loose and fracture the mat inside a shingle without leaving anything you would notice from the street. Months later the spot lets water through. Damage confined to one slope may be repairable; damage everywhere is a claim conversation. See hail damage roof repair.

Nails backing out

Decking swells and shrinks with heat and moisture, and over many seasons that movement can push a nail up until it lifts the shingle above it. The bump becomes a small hole. A repair pulls or resets the nail, seals the old hole and replaces the damaged shingle.

Worn valleys and ridge caps

Valleys carry the water from two slopes at once, and ridge caps sit in full sun from morning to evening. Both tend to wear out ahead of the field shingles. A worn valley can be rebuilt without touching the rest of the roof if the surrounding shingles are still flexible.

A roofer standing on your roof makes this call, not a chart. These are the signs most roofers weigh, so you can follow the reasoning and ask for photos of whatever they point to.

Permits for roof repair in the City of Dallas

The city's Development Services department publishes plain rules for storm and roof work. In short, small fixes go ahead without paperwork, while big ones need a permit pulled by a contractor the city has registered.

  • No permit: minor repairs to roofing or roof vents, and temporary tarps.
  • Re-roof permit: replacing large areas of a roof or the entire roof.
  • Building permit: replacing structural members such as rafters, roof joists or wall sections.
  • Who applies: a contractor, the owner or an agent may apply, but a registered contractor must be on the application before the permit is issued.
  • Owners doing their own work file a Homeowner's Exemption from Contractor Registration form instead of registering.

Questions go to the city at 214-948-4480. Plano, Fort Worth, Garland and the other cities in the metro write their own rules, and each town page notes what its city requires.

What a careful repair visit looks like

Your part is a phone call. The line asks a few questions and passes you to a roofing company; from there, a well-run repair tends to follow the same order.

  1. The roofer listens first. Where the stain is, when it drips, which way the wind was blowing, how old the roof is. Those answers narrow the search before anyone sets up a ladder.
  2. Roof and attic both get checked. Water travels along the deck and rafters before it drops, so the entry point is often uphill from the stain. A good roofer looks from below as well as above.
  3. You see what was found. Ask for photos of the failed boot, the gap in the flashing or the broken seal, taken that day.
  4. The estimate is written and itemized. Shingles by brand and line, flashing, boots, and decking priced per sheet if any turns up rotten. It should say whether a part is replaced or just sealed.
  5. The cause is fixed, not covered. New metal or a new boot where one failed. A smear of roof cement over a gap is a stopgap and should be priced and labeled as one.
  6. The area is cleaned up and nails swept from the beds and driveway, with the workmanship warranty, if any, written on the invoice.

Questions to ask before you say yes to a repair

About the work

  • What exactly failed, and can you show me a photo?
  • Will you replace the part or seal it, and how long should each option last?
  • Are matching shingles still made for my roof, and what happens if they are not?
  • Did you see the same problem anywhere else on the roof?

About who is doing it

  • Will your insurer email me a current liability certificate directly?
  • Are you registered with my city, if my city registers roofers?
  • What workmanship warranty comes with a repair, and is it in writing?
  • When do you expect payment, and is any deposit required for a small job?

Many roofers will look at a roof at no charge, though policies differ, so ask about that during the call.

Small habits that keep repairs small

{{ img('attic-roof-decking-flashlight-inspection.jpg', 'Person in an attic shining a flashlight along the roof rafters and decking', 1200, 800) }}

The National Roofing Contractors Association recommends a roof check at least twice a year, spring and fall, plus another after any severe storm, and it tells owners to look only from the ground. In practice that means walking around the house with your phone camera, looking for shingles in the yard, dark patches, sagging gutters or metal bent at the edges.

Indoors, glance at ceilings in upstairs rooms and closets after heavy rain. If the attic has a safe floored area by the hatch, a look with a flashlight from that spot can show a damp patch early. Leave anything beyond that to the roofer. Catching a split boot in April usually means a short repair; finding it after a summer of storms can mean new decking and drywall as well.
