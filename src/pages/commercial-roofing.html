Business roofs across Dallas are a separate trade

Drive any stretch of Stemmons Freeway, LBJ or Interstate 20 and nearly every roof you pass is close to flat: distribution warehouses, strip centers, office buildings, schools, churches and garden apartment complexes. Commercial roofing in Dallas is mostly low-slope roofing, where a continuous membrane or layered asphalt system keeps water out instead of overlapping shingles.

Those roofs need different skills. Seams are welded or adhered rather than nailed, water leaves through drains and scuppers rather than running off an eave, and much of the risk sits around air conditioning curbs, exhaust fans and parapet walls. A company that mostly re-shingles houses may not be the right call for a 60,000 square foot tilt-wall building.

Low-slope roof systems compared

Most commercial bids in DFW will propose one of the systems below, or a coating over the existing roof. The right one depends on the deck, the drainage, what sits on the roof, how long the owner plans to hold the building and what the budget allows. What North Texas weather does to a flat roof

Hail on exposed membranes

A low-slope roof has nothing shading it from a hail core. Hard hits can fracture brittle membranes, crush insulation boards underneath and crack the flashing on rooftop units. The damage often does not leak right away, which is why a post-storm walk by someone who knows the system matters.

Heat and sun

Long summers of strong sun and triple-digit afternoons bake the surface, age sealants and work seams back and forth as the roof expands by day and shrinks at night. Reflective membranes and coatings reduce that load and the cooling bill below.

Water that does not leave

Clogged drains, sagging decks and missing crickets behind units leave ponds that outlast the storm. Standing water adds weight, wears the surface and finds any weak seam. Gutters and scuppers full of debris after a windstorm make it worse.

Wind at the edges

Straight-line thunderstorm winds pull hardest at roof corners and perimeters. Loose edge metal or coping lets air under the membrane, and a sheet that starts to flutter can peel back across a large area.

The cool roof rule in the energy code

Energy code requirements reach the roof. Section C402.3 of the 2021 International Energy Conservation Code covers low-sloped roofs directly above cooled conditioned space in climate zones 0 through 3. Those roofs must meet one of two targets: a three-year aged solar reflectance of 0.55 together with a three-year aged thermal emittance of 0.75, or a three-year aged solar reflectance index of 64. Parts of a roof under solar panels, rooftop equipment, vegetative roofing, decks or stone or paver ballast are among the exemptions, as are roofs shaded at summer peak sun.

North Texas falls in those zones: the North Central Texas Council of Governments' regional amendments to the 2021 code describe the region as climate zones 2 and 3, and Dallas Building Inspection staff recommended adopting the 2021 code with those regional amendments. Each city adopts its own version, so the permit office has the final word.

  • Ask the bidder which code edition the city is enforcing and whether the proposed membrane meets C402.3 as amended there.
  • Ask for the product's listing in the Cool Roof Rating Council directory. ENERGY STAR stopped labeling roof products on June 1, 2022, so an ENERGY STAR claim on a roof product is out of date.
  • If a dark membrane or coating is proposed, ask which exemption makes it allowable.

Permits for a commercial roof in Dallas

Inside the City of Dallas, minor roof repairs do not need a permit, but replacing large areas of a roof or an entire roof does. A roofing permit can be obtained over the counter or online; the city's permit guide sends commercial applications to the Permit Center in the Oak Cliff Municipal Center, 320 E. Jefferson Blvd., Room 118, and building permit applications are now filed through the city's DallasNow portal. The application must name a contractor registered with the city.

Dallas adds a step many owners do not expect. Its 2015 amendments to the construction code administration chapter require a re-roof permit application to list where tear-off debris will be dumped, and the final inspection is not approved until proof of disposal at a City of Dallas landfill or transfer station is turned in. Ask the roofer who handles that paperwork.

Structural work, such as replacing damaged joists or deck, can require a building permit rather than a roofing permit. Suburbs run their own offices with their own rules; Plano, for example, requires a permit for every re-roof.

Six things to settle with every commercial bidder

  1. What is under the membrane? Ask for core cuts or a moisture survey before anyone proposes a coating or a recover over the old roof.
  2. Why this system? Have the bidder explain the choice in terms of your deck, drainage, rooftop equipment and how long you plan to own the building.
  3. How will water leave the roof? Drains, scuppers, overflow paths and any tapered insulation or crickets should be on the drawing.
  4. Which warranty, from whom? Separate the manufacturer's system warranty from the contractor's workmanship warranty, and read what each excludes, including hail.
  5. Who pulls the permit and registers with the city? Get the permit number and the inspection dates.
  6. How will tenants and customers be protected? Work hours, odors, noise, crane days, parking and fire safety for any torch work belong in the schedule.

Insist on proof of liability coverage that comes straight from the roofer's insurance agent, listing your company as the certificate holder.

Keeping a business roof working between projects

NRCA's guidance for building owners is to schedule inspections at least two times a year, with spring and fall the usual seasons, and to add one after severe weather such as hail or a tornado. NRCA also cautions that owners and property staff should not walk a roof without the experience and equipment to do it safely, and that repairs found on an inspection should go to a roofing contractor promptly. In practice, the roof's to-do list looks like this:

  • Keep drains, strainers and scuppers clear, especially after windstorms and in fall.
  • Log every roof access: HVAC techs and other trades are a common source of punctures.
  • Keep photo records from each inspection to support any future hail or wind claim.
  • Fix small problems at seams, curbs and edges before they wet the insulation.
