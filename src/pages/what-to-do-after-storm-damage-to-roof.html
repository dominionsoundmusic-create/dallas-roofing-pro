What counts as storm damage

Storm damage to a roof is harm caused by a particular weather event: hail that bruised shingles or dented metal, wind that lifted, creased or tore shingles away, or a tree limb or flying debris that cracked or punched through the roof. Damage that built up slowly, such as sun-baked shingles that curl, brittle sealant around a pipe, or a roof that simply reached the end of its life, is wear, not storm damage.

The difference matters twice. It tells a roofer whether a repair or a full replacement makes sense, and it shapes whether a homeowners claim makes sense at all. Insurers generally look for sudden damage tied to a date, so knowing when the storm hit and what it left behind is the first thing to write down.

North Texas gives people plenty of practice. The EF3 tornado that crossed North Dallas on October 20, 2019, the December 26, 2015 tornado through Sunnyvale, Garland and Rowlett, and the April 11, 2016 hailstorm that the National Weather Service says affected about 80 percent of homes in Wylie all left whole streets of damaged roofs behind.

Hail damage on shingles is often invisible from below. Bruises, cracked mats and loosened granules can sit right next to undamaged shingles and only show up on a close inspection. That is one reason to get a roofer's opinion even when the roof looks fine from the driveway, especially after a storm that dented cars or siding nearby.

After the storm: hour, day, week, month

Most of what goes wrong after a DFW storm comes from rushing the wrong step or skipping a dull one. Work through this in order.

The first hour: people first

  • Stay inside until the storm has fully passed; another cell may follow the first.
  • Keep away from downed or sagging power lines and anything touching them. Report them to Oncor or your electric provider, or call 911.
  • If you smell gas, leave the house and call 911 from outside.
  • Inside, move furniture and electronics out from under drips, set out buckets and towels, and do not let anyone stand under a ceiling that bulges.
  • Stay off the roof and leave the ladder in the garage.

The first day: record and report

  • Note the date and time the storm hit and the size of any hail you saw. A photo of stones next to a coin or ruler helps.
  • Photograph the roof from the ground, the yard debris, dents in gutters and vents, and every stain, drip and wet spot inside.
  • Call your insurer and write down the claim number and the name of the person you spoke with.
  • Call a roofer to look at the roof and, if it is open, to cover it.

The first week: protect and compare

  • Have a roofer tarp any opening. TDI tells homeowners to make temporary repairs that prevent more damage and to keep the receipts, but to hold off on permanent repairs until the insurer has seen the damage.
  • Meet the adjuster, ideally with your roofer present, and ask for a copy of the adjuster's estimate.
  • Get a second written estimate if the first roofer's scope seems thin or the price seems far off.
  • Check any company that knocked on your door before signing anything.

The first month: decide and finish

  • Compare the insurer's estimate with the roofer's line by line and ask the insurer about anything missing.
  • Confirm the permit your city requires. In Dallas, replacing large areas or the whole roof needs a re-roof permit; a tarp and minor roof repairs do not.
  • You owe the deductible: pay it, keep proof, and get the deductible notice in any insurance-funded contract of $1,000 or more.
  • Keep the final invoice, permit sign-off and warranty papers together with your claim file.

Keeping the damage from spreading

{{ img('ceiling-leak-bucket-living-room.jpg', 'Water stain on a living room ceiling with a bucket on the floor below and furniture covered in plastic', 1200, 800) }}

Insurers expect homeowners to take reasonable steps so a storm problem does not turn into a bigger one. In practice that means two things you can do and one you should hire out.

What you can do: catch the water and protect what is under it. Buckets, towels and plastic sheeting over furniture cost little and save a lot. If a ceiling bulges with water, keep people and pets out of that room and away from light fixtures in it.

What to hire out: covering the roof. A roofer can put a tarp over the damaged area and fasten it so wind does not tear it loose. Ask for an itemized receipt that lists the tarp work separately; it belongs in your claim file. The City of Dallas states that a tarp or similar temporary measure needs no permit, so it can go on right away.

Then leave the permanent repair until the adjuster has looked, as TDI advises. If water got into walls, insulation or flooring, tell the insurer that as well; wet materials can cause problems long after the roof is fixed.

Door knockers and storm chasers

After a big hail day in DFW, crews from other places often show up quickly, and some move on once the work dries up. Plenty of door-to-door roofers do honest work, but the pitch often moves fast. Slow it down.

Ask for the city permit

Several DFW cities make door-to-door sellers carry a permit. McKinney requires one any time someone solicits in the city, good for six months. Saginaw requires a permit and badge, respects No Soliciting signs, and limits hours to 9 a.m. to 8 p.m. from April to October and 9 a.m. to 6 p.m. from November to March. Check your own city's rules.

Know your three days

The Texas Attorney General explains that, in many cases, you can back out of a contract signed at your door for three business days afterward, and that the seller must inform you of this right. Never let anyone rush you past it, and never sign a blank or half-filled form.

Watch the money

After a declared disaster, Business and Commerce Code Chapter 58 bars a contractor without a local address of at least a year from requiring payment before work begins. Do not sign your insurance check over to anyone, and turn away anyone who offers to absorb the deductible.

Your storm file: what to keep

A shoebox or a folder on your phone works. What matters is that nothing gets thrown away until the claim is closed and the roof is finished.

  • Photos and video from the day of the storm and the days after, with dates
  • Claim number, adjuster's name and contact, and notes from every call
  • Receipts for tarps, buckets, plastic, water removal and any hotel stay
  • Every written estimate, from your roofer and from the insurer
  • The signed contract, the permit number and the final invoice
  • Proof you paid the deductible, which your insurer may ask for
  • Shingle warranty registration and the roofer's workmanship warranty
